By calculating TCI at the corporate level, we explore whether it is possible to capture more granular insights into technological capabilities within regions. By comparing TCI results derived from regional and corporate approaches, we assess how classification granularity influences the evaluation of inequality and stability of technological complexity across regions and corporations (Panel A in Fig.~\ref{fig:detailtci}).
In this study, we address these inconsistencies by comparing TCI results obtained from regional and corporate approaches, analyzing how classification granularity affects inequality and stability of complexity across regions and corporations (Panel A in Fig.~\ref{fig:detailtci}).

The scaled TCI of the Schmoch system shows that in the regional analysis 26 out of 35 classes fall in the range of 75 to 100 (Fig.~\ref{fig:detailtci}). 
In the corporate analysis, only 15 classes lie in that range, whereas the other classes display greater differences among technologies in the regional analysis. 
This difference stems from the corporate analysis being able to characterize each technology in a more detailed manner. 
The HH algorithm defines ubiquity as the degree of a technology node and this measure depends on the number of paired nodes (i.e., regions or corporations). 
The regional analysis comprised roughly a few hundred region nodes, such as countries or EU cities\cite{Hidalgo2021,Daniel2020technological}.
The corporate analysis has corporation nodes numbering from one thousand to several ten thousand. The iterative calculation of TCI can therefore reflect a wider range of ubiquity.
The difference between the approaches also appears when using a finer classification. When the classification becomes more detailed and the number of technology nodes increases, the regional approach cannot define ubiquity sufficiently. 
In the case of Civil engineering the variance of TCI values for IPC classes corresponding to the same Schmoch class is larger than that for the corporate approach. 
The larger difference between the TCI values of the coarse classification and those of the finer classification indicates a greater inconsistency in the evaluation for the regional approach. 
A Mann-Whitney U test shows that the distribution of residuals between the TCI values of the Schmoch and IPC classes is significantly different (U = 7495.5, p value = 6.55e-15, r value = 0.60) and that the residuals for the corporate analysis are smaller than those for the regional approach (Panel B in Fig.~\ref{fig:detailtci}).
These consistent and detailed trends are observed not only over a 30 year period but also in evaluations over shorter periods such as 5 years. For example, in the IPC classes corresponding to Food chemistry, the trend of high TCI is consistent in the 2000s. 
Technologies with high scores as C13 and A21 and those that decline as A23 and A01 are captured in a consistent manner (SI Fig.~\ref{fig:bump}).

Moreover, in terms of comparison between the portfolios of specific regions, regional analysis has compared connections features such as average TCI or whether there is a link to a specific technology. 
Conversely, the corporate analysis in this study allows for the comparison of each technology's TCI among corporate networks in different regions. 
This analysis therefore offers a quantitative perspective for for capturing the technological portfolio of a specific region.
For instance, Fig.~\ref{fig:prefcompare} presents a comparative analysis of corporate TCI values for IPC classes among Tokyo, Osaka, and Aichi, shown pairwise as Tokyo–Osaka (left), Tokyo–Aichi (middle), and Osaka–Aichi (right), the three prefectures with the highest regional GDP.
The plot for Tokyo and Osaka (left panel) suggests a strong correlation between corporate TCI in Tokyo and corporate TCI in Osaka. The relatively tight clustering of data points along the $\text{y} = \text{x}$ diagonal implies that Tokyo's technological structure closely resembles that of Osaka. A few outliers can be observed, including Combinatorial Chemistry (C40), which appears to have a markedly lower TCI in both regions, and Weapons (F41), where Osaka records a relatively higher TCI that may be associated with its traditional cutlery industry.
Similarly, the plot for Tokyo and Aichi (middle panel) indicates a comparable overall correlation, but with noticeably greater dispersion. Several technologies in electronics and ICT, such as computing (G06), basic electronic circuits (H03), and telecommunications (H04), appear above the diagonal, suggesting that their complexity in Aichi may be relatively higher than in Tokyo. In contrast, classes such as hand or travelling articles (A45), lighting (F21), and bookbinding/printing-related technologies (B42) are located below the diagonal, implying a relative advantage for Tokyo in these consumer-goods and light manufacturing fields.
In contrast, the Osaka–Aichi comparison (right panel) appears to exhibit the most pronounced divergence. While many ICT-related classes such as G03, G04, G08, G09, H03, and H04 again lie above the diagonal in Aichi, Osaka seems to have higher complexity in manufacturing-oriented classes such as presses (B30), decorative arts (B44), hand or travelling articles (A45), and lighting (F21). This pattern may highlight that Aichi’s technological profile diverges not only from Tokyo but also from Osaka, reflecting a distinctive regional specialization in electronics and ICT.
The corporate analysis thus suggests that Aichi possesses a unique technological structure, characterized by a relatively strong specialization that may differ from the profiles of Tokyo and Osaka.
