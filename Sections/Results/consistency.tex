\subsection*{Consistency over Time and Comparison between Regions}
\phantomsection
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Consistency}
\label{subsection:consistency}

The preceding section summarized TCI over the full period from fiscal year 1981 to 2010 and provided a baseline view of technological characteristics in Japan. 
In this section, we examine how stable that structure may be over time. For policy and strategy, an important consideration is not only which technologies appear complex on average but also whether their relative positions tend to persist or show signs of reordering as the innovation system evolves. At the same time, it has been pointed out that TCI may be sensitive to design choices such as the granularity of the technology classification and the length of the time window\cite{PintarEssletzbichler2022}. 
Therefore, we compute TCI for the Schmoch 35 classes in six consecutive five year windows spanning fiscal year 1981 to 2010, and rank technologies by TCI within each window(Fig.~\ref{fig:detailtci}).

An analysis of the temporal ranking suggests that the technological hierarchy exhibits considerable persistence, with a stratified overall structure (Fig.~\ref{fig:detailtci}). Distinct groups of technologies tend to occupy the top and bottom tiers over the 30-year period. Within this general stability, certain dynamics are also apparent, including periods of substantial reordering.

A closer examination of the figure reveals several patterns. First, a group of technologies related to chemistry and life sciences is consistently positioned in the top ranks. These include Pharmaceuticals, Food chemistry, Organic fine chemistry, Biotechnology, and Macromolecular chemistry, polymers.

Conversely, technologies associated with ICT are generally found in the lower ranks. Fields such as Digital communication, Computer technology, Telecommunications, and IT methods for management remain in the lower portion of the ranking across most windows. The middle ranks are characterized by more volatility, with several mechanical and electrical engineering fields showing fluctuations. The relative positions of Electrical machinery, apparatus, energy, Semiconductors, Measurement, Mechanical elements, and Machine tools, for example, shift over time.

Regarding the temporal dynamics, a notable degree of reordering occurs between the 1980s and the early 1990s. In the 2000s, the rankings show signs of stabilization. While the high-ranking chemical and medical technologies solidify their positions, some fields exhibit distinct trajectories. For example, Semiconductors and Micro-structural and nano-technology experience a temporary rise in rank before a subsequent downward adjustment. The initial high rank of Micro-structural and nano-technology coincides with a period when few corporations possessed production capabilities in this field.

These consistent trends are observed not only 35 Schmoch classes but also IPC classes which are more finer classification (SI Fig.~\ref{fig:bump}).
For example, in the IPC classes related to Food Chemistry, a persistently high TCI is observed for sugars (C13) and baking technologies (A21) throughout the 2000s.
By contrast, foodstuffs (A23) and agriculture-related technologies (A01) show gradual declines in ranking, particularly after the mid-1990s, indicating that even within related technological fields, some areas maintain their complexity advantage while others lose ground.
This suggests that the relative positioning of technologies can be captured in a stable and systematic manner across different time resolutions.

Regional comparisons are also informative, since similarity or divergence in corporate TCI across prefectures can reveal distinctive industrial bases and specialization patterns. We compare cross prefectural alignment by pairing Tokyo, Osaka, and Aichi and reading class level correspondence from concentration around the diagonal and salient departures in the scatter plots (Fig.~\ref{fig:prefcompare}). Tokyo and Osaka exhibit a close correspondence across many fields. Tokyo and Aichi show a looser association in which Aichi tends to be stronger in electronics and ICT, for example Computing (G06) and Telecommunications (H04), while Tokyo shows relative strengths in consumer goods and light manufacturing, for example Printing and Bookbinding (B42) and Lighting Technology (F21). The pair of Osaka and Aichi diverges the most, with Aichi again relatively strong in electronics, for example Computing (G06) and Basic Electronic Circuitry (H03), whereas Osaka appears higher in manufacturing oriented areas, for example Presses (B30) and Decorative Arts and Processes (B44). This pattern may highlight that Aichi’s technological profile diverges not only from Tokyo but also from Osaka, reflecting a distinctive regional specialization in electronics and ICT.
The corporate analysis thus may make finer regional technological characteristics visible within each prefecture than the regional level analysis.
